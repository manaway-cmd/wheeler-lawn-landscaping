What can go wrong over winter

Even a properly winterized system takes a season of freeze-thaw cycling, settling ground, and idle valves. A head can crack from a hard frost heaving the soil around it, a valve can seize from months of not moving, or a controller battery can die and wipe the schedule. Start-up catches these before the system runs blind on autopilot.

How we bring a system back online

  1. Slow pressurization. We open the main valve gradually to avoid a pressure spike hitting old fittings.
  2. Backflow check. Inspect the device for cracks from anything that froze despite winterizing.
  3. Zone-by-zone walk. Run each zone and check every head for spray pattern, coverage, and leaks.
  4. Controller check. Confirm the program matches the season and the battery backup is good.
  5. Adjustments. Nozzles and arcs get reset where heads have drifted or settled.

Frequently asked questions

When should I turn my sprinkler system on in spring?

Once overnight lows are reliably staying above freezing, which is usually early-to-mid April around Lee's Summit. Turning the water back on before then risks a late frost catching water in the lines again, undoing the point of winterizing.

What do you check during spring start-up?

Water pressure at the main, every zone and head for winter damage, the backflow device for cracks, and the controller program against the current season. Anything found gets flagged before you're relying on a system that's only partly working.

How often should sprinkler heads be adjusted or replaced?

Check spray patterns every spring since heads shift from freeze-thaw movement, mower contact, and normal settling. Most heads last five to ten years before the internals wear out and the spray gets uneven or weak.

Do you offer same-week service calls?

During spring start-up season we book by the week rather than same-day, since everyone's system needs attention at once. Call or text early in the season for the shortest wait.
